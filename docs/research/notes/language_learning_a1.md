# Evidence-based design of an A1 French and Spanish self-study course (English-speaking adult, daily-practice app)

Research date: 2026-11-24. Reading notes for the report writer:

- "Cited Findings" are things I read in a source during this run. Where I only saw a search-result summary and not the page itself, the bullet says "(search summary only)".
- "Inferences" are my design recommendations or standard teaching knowledge that I did not source in this run. Treat them as proposals, not facts.
- Two primary sources could not be read: the official Plan Curricular del Instituto Cervantes (PCIC) pages (cvc.cervantes.es redirected to a maintenance page) and the France Éducation International "Inventaire linguistique" PDF. Both are represented by secondary documents that apply them.

## 1. What does CEFR A1 cover, and what is the concrete A1 inventory for French and Spanish (10-14 unit order)?

### Takeaway
A1 is "familiar everyday expressions and very basic phrases for concrete needs". In both languages that means present tense plus a near-future periphrasis, articles/gender/number agreement, basic questions and negation, numbers/time, and personal-information functions. French A1 additionally includes a limited passé composé, which Spanish A1 (per the UCM programme based on the PCIC) does not match with any past tense.

### Cited Findings

CEFR level definition
- A1 global-scale descriptor: "Can understand and use familiar everyday expressions and very basic phrases aimed at the satisfaction of needs of a concrete type." — [Council of Europe, CEFR global scale](https://www.coe.int/en/web/common-european-framework-reference-languages/table-1-cefr-3.3-common-reference-levels-global-scale) (wording seen via search summary of this page and university copies, e.g. [Durham PDF](https://www.dur.ac.uk/media/durham-university/research-/research-centres/centre-for-foreign-language-study/downloads/CEFR.pdf))
- The descriptors were updated and extended in the 2020 CEFR Companion Volume; I did not verify whether the A1 global wording changed (search summary only). — [CoE descriptor search](https://www.coe.int/web/common-european-framework-reference-languages/cefr-descriptors-search)

French: DELF A1 (exam shape, useful as a target for what an A1 learner must be able to do)
- Four papers, each marked out of 25: listening (4 exercises, 20 min), reading (4 exercises, 30 min), writing (2 exercises, 30 min: fill in a form and write a message of at least 40 words), speaking (3 parts, 5-7 min, with 10 min preparation). Pass = 50/100 with at least 5/25 per paper (search summary only). — [France Éducation International, DELF tout public A1](https://www.france-education-international.fr/diplome/delf-tout-public/niveau-a1)
- The official DELF A1 pages say the candidate can conjugate the most common verbs in the present and the futur proche, and must pay attention to masculine/feminine and singular/plural agreement (search summary only). — [France Éducation International, DELF A1](https://www.france-education-international.fr/diplome/delf-tout-public/niveau-a1)
- There is no legally fixed DELF grammar syllabus. The closest reference is FEI's "Inventaire linguistique des contenus clés des niveaux du CECRL", a teacher reference tool, not an exam programme. — [digiSchool, "Grammaire du DELF A1, A2, B1 et B2"](https://www.digischool.fr/articles/fle/grammaire-delf-a1-a2-b1-b2/)

French A1 grammar inventory (as listed by digiSchool from the FEI inventory; the page does not separate "key" from "less key" items) — [digiSchool](https://www.digischool.fr/articles/fle/grammaire-delf-a1-a2-b1-b2/)
- Verbs: present of regular and common verbs; être en train de; futur proche (aller + infinitive); passé composé with avoir, and with être for a few verbs only; passé récent (venir de + infinitive); devoir/pouvoir/vouloir + infinitive; imperative for a few verbs; polite conditional as fixed phrases (je voudrais, j'aimerais); pronominal verbs; pour + infinitive; il faut / il ne faut pas + infinitive.
- Questions: qui est-ce ?, qu'est-ce que c'est ?; three question forms (inversion, est-ce que, question word first); qui, où, quand, quel/quelle, combien, comment, pourquoi.
- Negation: ne... pas, ne... jamais, and "pas" alone in speech.
- Presentatives: il y a, c'est, ce sont, voici, voilà.
- Nouns and determiners: gender and number; definite, indefinite and partitive articles; quantity expressions (un peu de, beaucoup de, pas de); intensity adverbs (très, trop); adjective position and agreement; possessive and demonstrative adjectives.
- Prepositions/adverbs/pronouns: place (à, au, de, chez, avec, dans; ici, là); time (à 7 h, en 2015; maintenant, bientôt, demain; dans, depuis, il y a); subject and stressed pronouns; connectors et, ou, mais, alors.
- Placed at A2, so out of scope: futur simple; imparfait and the passé composé/imparfait contrast; participle agreement; ne... plus/rien/personne; comparatives and superlatives; object pronouns (COD/COI), en, y; relative pronouns qui/que/où.

Spanish A1 grammar inventory (Universidad Complutense de Madrid A1 programme, split into A1.1 and A1.2; a Spanish-university syllabus built on the PCIC, not the PCIC itself) — [UCM, "Programa de gramática nivel A1"](https://www.ucm.es/data/cont/media/www/pag-41464/a1_gramatica.pdf)
- A1.1 nouns: proper names, forms of address (Sr., Sra., Don, Doña), country names without article; gender (-o/-a, consonant/-a, invariable nouns, exceptions); plural in -s and -es.
- A1.1 adjectives: agreement with the noun, position after the noun, nationality adjectives.
- A1.1 determiners: definite article (regular paradigm, contractions, article with gustar); indefinite article; demonstratives (masc./fem., sing./plural, near/far); unstressed possessives; cardinal numbers and ordinals to 10th.
- A1.1 pronouns: subject pronouns including usted/ustedes; direct-object pronouns; indirect-object le/les; reflexive pronouns.
- A1.1 interrogatives: qué, quién/quiénes, cuánto/a/os/as, dónde, cómo, por qué.
- A1.1 adverbs: aquí, ahí, allí; ahora, hoy, mañana; poco, mucho; sí, no.
- A1.1 verb: present indicative, regular and irregular (ser, estar, haber, ir); infinitive; regular participle.
- A1.1 ser/estar/hay: ser for identification, origin, profession, material; estar for location and with bien/mal; estar with definite article/possessives/proper names versus hay with indefinite article, numerals and bare plurals.
- A1.1 sentences: affirmative and negative statements; yes/no questions; coordination with y, ni, o; subordinate clauses with infinitive after ser and gustar, creer/pensar + present, relative que, porque + present, para + infinitive.
- A1.2 additions: gender of inanimate nouns and more exceptions; superlative in -ísimo and muy; gustar-type verbs (encantar, molestar); un (new referent) vs el (known referent); neuter demonstratives; omission of subject pronouns unless emphatic; se before direct-object pronouns; por qué vs porque; bastante, también/tampoco, mucho/muy; present with durative value; more ser uses (time, identification); estar/hay with place adverbs; wh-questions; pero; querer/pedir + infinitive; decir/contar + present; entonces.
- No past tense and no future tense form appears in the UCM A1.1/A1.2 verb list; the only tense is the present indicative. — [UCM](https://www.ucm.es/data/cont/media/www/pag-41464/a1_gramatica.pdf)
- Other A1 syllabi built on the PCIC likewise list present indicative with the irregulars ser/estar/haber and the ser/estar distinction (professions and countries versus adjectives and places) (search summary only). — [InHispania A1 syllabus](https://inhispania.com/es/curso-nivel-a1/)
- UCM also publishes a matching A1 vocabulary programme, which I did not read. — [UCM a1_lexico.pdf](https://www.ucm.es/data/cont/media/www/pag-41464/a1_lexico.pdf)

### Inferences
Proposed 12-unit order for each language (my synthesis of the inventories above; the ordering itself is not from a source). Functions first, grammar attached to the function that needs it.

French (A1)
1. Sounds and survival phrases: alphabet, bonjour/salut/merci/s'il vous plaît, tu vs vous, je m'appelle.
2. Who I am: être, subject pronouns, nationalities/professions, c'est / il est.
3. What I have: avoir, age (j'ai 30 ans), numbers 0-69, il y a.
4. Things and people: gender, un/une/des, le/la/les, plural, family, possessives (mon/ma/mes).
5. Describing: adjective agreement and position, colours, très/trop, demonstratives (ce/cette/ces).
6. Daily actions: -er verbs in the present, ne... pas, aimer + noun/infinitive.
7. Asking: est-ce que, intonation questions, qui/où/quand/comment/combien/pourquoi, quel.
8. Time: numbers 70-100+, clock, days, months, dates, à/en/dans, pronominal verbs (se lever).
9. Places and movement: aller, venir, à/au/chez/dans/de, contractions (au, du), city and directions vocabulary.
10. Food and shopping: partitives (du/de la/des, pas de), quantities, je voudrais, prendre/boire/manger, prices.
11. Plans and obligations: futur proche, pouvoir/vouloir/devoir, il faut, a few imperatives.
12. What happened: passé composé with avoir (a short verb list), a handful of être verbs, venir de, hier/la semaine dernière.

Spanish (A1)
1. Sounds and survival phrases: alphabet, the five vowels, ¿ ¡, hola/gracias/por favor, tú vs usted, me llamo.
2. Who I am: ser, subject pronouns (and dropping them), nationality/profession/origin.
3. Things and people: gender (-o/-a), el/la/los/las, un/una, plural, family, possessives (mi/tu/su).
4. Describing: adjective agreement and position, colours, muy/mucho, demonstratives.
5. Where and how: estar for location and state, hay vs estar, aquí/ahí/allí, numbers 0-100.
6. Daily actions: regular -ar/-er/-ir present, no + verb, tener (age, tener que).
7. Asking: qué, quién, dónde, cómo, cuánto, por qué/porque, yes/no questions.
8. Time: clock (es la una / son las dos), days, months, dates, reflexive routine verbs (levantarse).
9. Likes: gustar/encantar with me/te/le, también/tampoco, food vocabulary.
10. Common irregulars: ir, querer, poder, hacer, stem changes (e>ie, o>ue) as patterns, not tables.
11. Shopping and getting around: prices, quiero/quisiera, direct-object pronouns lo/la, directions, para + infinitive, basic por/para.
12. Plans: ir a + infinitive, porque/pero/entonces, review. (Optional 13th "preview" unit: a few fixed past phrases, since past tenses are A2 in the UCM programme.)

- French needs one more grammar topic than Spanish at A1 (limited passé composé), so the French pack should either run one unit longer or thin out vocabulary in unit 12.
- "ir a + infinitive" is not in the UCM A1 list I read; I include it because it is built only from A1 pieces (ir + a + infinitive) and mirrors French futur proche. Flag as a design choice, not a syllabus requirement.

### Gaps
- Official PCIC A1 inventory (grammar, functions, "nociones específicas" vocabulary): cvc.cervantes.es redirected to a maintenance page on 2026-11-24, so the Spanish inventory rests on the UCM programme only.
- The FEI "Inventaire linguistique" PDF and Beacco's "Niveau A1 pour le français" référentiel were not read directly; the French list is second-hand from digiSchool.
- I did not retrieve the detailed A1 can-do scales (listening, reading, spoken interaction, writing) from the Companion Volume, only the global descriptor.

## 2. How many words does A1 need, and what does frequency research say?

### Takeaway
The first 1,000 lemmas give about 76-80% coverage of written Spanish and about 88% of spoken Spanish, with sharply diminishing returns after that; a 500-700 word A1 pack drawn from the top of the frequency list plus survival topic words is therefore defensible, but I found no authoritative source that fixes A1 at 500-700 words.

### Cited Findings
- Spanish token coverage by frequency band (Davies' 20-million-word corpus): first 1,000 lemmas cover 76.0% of non-fiction, 79.6% of fiction, 87.8% of oral text; second 1,000 add 8.0 / 6.5 / 4.9 points; third 1,000 add 4.2 / 3.5 / 2.3; first 3,000 total 88.2% / 89.6% / 94.0%. — [Davies, "Vocabulary Range and Text Coverage"](https://www.mark-davies.org/articles/davies_26.pdf)
- Davies' conclusion: 1,000 words let a learner recognize 75-80% of lexemes in written Spanish and about 88% in spoken Spanish; there is "a law of diminishing returns" beyond that. — [Davies](https://www.mark-davies.org/articles/davies_26.pdf)
- Coverage differs by part of speech: in spoken Spanish the most frequent 10% of noun lemmas (1,289 nouns) cover 79.3% of noun tokens, while the top 10% of verb lemmas (231 verbs) cover 90.6% of verb tokens. — [Davies, Table 5](https://www.mark-davies.org/articles/davies_26.pdf)
- The second edition of Davies' "A Frequency Dictionary of Spanish" is based on a web corpus of more than 2 billion words from 21 Spanish-speaking countries (search summary only). — [Routledge](https://routledge.com/9781138686540)
- Lonsdale & Le Bras, "A Frequency Dictionary of French" (Routledge, 2009), lists the 5,000 most frequent French words from a 23-million-word corpus balanced across written and spoken, literary and non-literary, France and other regions (mainly Canada); ranking weighs frequency together with range (search summary only). — [Lextutor Vocabprofil note](https://lextutor.ca/vp/comp/lonsd.html)
- For comparison, unassisted comprehension at 98% coverage needs 8,000-9,000 word families for written English and 6,000-7,000 for spoken English. — [Nation 2006](https://www.wgtn.ac.nz/__data/assets/pdf_file/0018/1626120/2006-How-large-a-vocab.pdf)
- Milton & Alexiou (2009) map XLex yes/no recognition scores to CEFR levels; the search summary reports an A1 figure of about 1,160 words for French and under 1,500 for English. This is a recognition measure on a 5,000-word scale, and the table was truncated in what I saw (search summary only). — [Milton & Alexiou](https://www.enl.auth.gr/gala/14th/Papers/English%20papers/Milton&Alexiou.pdf)

### Inferences
- Because verbs concentrate much more than nouns (231 verbs cover about 91% of spoken verb tokens), the pack should front-load a small set of very frequent verbs and teach them thoroughly, and choose nouns by topic need.
- Suggested order of word categories (my proposal, consistent with the coverage data but not from a single source): (1) function words: pronouns, articles, prepositions, conjunctions, question words, negation; (2) the top 30-50 verbs (be, have, go, want, can, do, say, know, like, need...); (3) numbers, days, months, time words; (4) high-frequency adjectives and adverbs; (5) concrete topic nouns for the A1 situations (family, food, home, city, work, travel); (6) fixed politeness and survival chunks, which are needed on day one regardless of frequency rank.
- Target size: roughly 600-700 lemmas per language over 12 units (about 50-60 per unit). The recognition-based research figure (about 1,160 for French A1) is higher than typical course word lists, so 600-700 active words plus cognate recognition is a reasonable compromise; state this as a design decision.
- English-French and English-Spanish cognates give a beginner many "free" recognition words; mark cognates in the pack and spend less practice on them, while flagging common false friends.

### Gaps
- No source found for the commonly quoted "A1 = 500-700 words". The search summary also mentioned a Milton claim that learners with 500-800 words cannot progress beyond A1, but I could not identify the document, so it is unverified.
- No French coverage percentages (top 100/1,000/2,000) from Lonsdale & Le Bras were found; the only band figures I have are Spanish (Davies) and English/German comparisons in the same paper.
- Top-100-word coverage for either language was not found.

## 3. What does learning science say about exercise formats?

### Takeaway
Retrieval practice and spacing are the two best-supported techniques; items should keep being tested after the first correct answer, typed recall should dominate once an item is introduced, and grammar contrasts should be mixed across sessions, not drilled in separate blocks.

### Cited Findings
- Karpicke & Roediger (2008, Science): students learned 40 Swahili-English pairs. One week later, conditions with repeated testing recalled about 80% of pairs; conditions that dropped items from testing after one correct recall scored 36% and 33%. "Repeated study after one successful recall did not produce any measurable learning a week later." — [Karpicke & Roediger 2008](https://www.brucehayes.org/Teaching/papers/2008_Roediger_Karpicke_Science.pdf)
- Same study: 40 students, 10 per condition, computerized flashcard format. — [Purdue news release](https://www.purdue.edu/uns/x/2008a/080214KarpickeTest.html)
- Dunlosky et al. (2013) rated practice testing and distributed practice as the two "high utility" techniques, working across formats, content, learner ages and retention intervals (search summary of the authors' Scientific American Mind summary). — [Dunlosky et al., SciAm Mind summary](https://pepper-tmp.oise.utoronto.ca/data/note/221966/Dunlosky_SciAmMind.pdf)
- Spacing: Cepeda et al. found the optimal gap was about 20% of the test delay in some conditions, but the ratio is not constant as the retention interval grows; "10-20% of the retention interval" is a rough heuristic only (search summary only). — [Cepeda et al., "Spacing effects in learning: a temporal ridgeline of optimal retention"](https://home.cs.colorado.edu/~mozer/Research/Selected%20Publications/reprints/Cepedaetal2009.pdf)
- Recall vs recognition formats: Nakata compared recognition, recall, hybrid and productive-recall-only conditions and found recall formats more effective than recognition for productive knowledge of spelling, while recognition did better on some other outcomes (search summary only). — [Nakata, IRAL](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/iral-2015-0022/html?lang=en)
- Productive vs receptive direction: productive learning (L1 to L2) tends to produce greater productive knowledge than receptive learning (secondary summary citing Mondria & Wiersma 2004 and others; search summary only). — [UWE repository paper](https://uwe-repository.worktribe.com/OutputFile/869191)
- Interleaving grammar: Pan et al. (2019, J. Educational Psychology), four experiments on Spanish preterite vs imperfect conjugation. Within a single session, interleaving gave no advantage (blocked was numerically higher in Exp. 1, equal in Exp. 2 on a two-day delayed test). Across two weekly sessions, interleaving gave "substantially better performance on a one-week delayed test". The authors call it the first demonstration of an interleaving effect in foreign language learning. — [Pan, Tajran, Lovelett, Osuna & Rickard 2019](https://sc-pan.github.io/pdf/PTLOR_2019W.pdf)
- Half-life regression (Settles & Meeder 2016): a spaced-repetition model fitted on Duolingo data cut recall-prediction error by more than 45% against baselines and raised daily engagement by 12% in a live study (search summary only). — [Settles & Meeder, ACL 2016](https://preview.aclanthology.org/landing_page/P16-1174)
- Duolingo's blog splits that effect as a 9.5% increase in next-day retention for practice sessions and 1.7% for lessons (search summary only). — [Duolingo blog](https://blog.duolingo.com/how-we-learn-how-you-learn)

### Inferences
- Do not retire an item after one correct answer. The Karpicke & Roediger result (about 80% vs 33-36%) is the single strongest argument for keeping "known" items in the review queue.
- Format ladder per item (my proposal): first exposure with audio and an example sentence; then multiple choice L2 to L1 (recognition, low failure); then typed L2 to L1; then typed L1 to L2 (productive); then cloze in a sentence; then listen-and-type. Promote on success, demote on failure. Multiple choice is a warm-up step only, never the mastery criterion.
- Grade typed answers leniently on accents at first (accept with a correction shown), strictly later; otherwise typed recall punishes keyboard skill instead of memory.
- Schedule: expanding intervals such as same session, +1 day, +3, +7, +16, +35 days. This is a conventional choice consistent with the spacing literature, not an interval set taken from a paper.
- Interleave within review sessions (mix ser/estar, le/la, tu/vous, -er/-ir verbs) but introduce each new point in a short blocked burst first; Pan et al. found interleaving helped across sessions, not during first exposure.
- Sentence-level practice: each word should carry at least one short example sentence, reused for cloze and dictation. I did not source the context-versus-isolated-word literature in this run (see Gaps).

### Gaps
- No sourced findings in this run for: audio/listening and pronunciation benefits, sentence context vs isolated words, cloze deletion specifically, Bjork's "desirable difficulties" primary papers, or Nation's own guidance on word-card learning. These are widely cited ideas but need sources before being stated as evidence.
- Effect sizes from Dunlosky et al. 2013 and exact numbers from Pan et al. 2019 and Nakata were not extracted.
- I found no Nakata & Webb meta-analysis on retrieval formats; the search suggested one may not exist under that description.

## 4. Ideal daily session length and new-item load; hours to A1

### Takeaway
Institutional estimates put A1 at roughly 60-100 guided hours, so 15-20 minutes a day reaches A1 in about 6-12 months; a load of about 5-10 new items a day keeps reviews manageable, given Anki's rule of thumb that daily reviews settle at about ten times the daily new-card count.

### Cited Findings
- Alliance Française: a complete beginner needs 60-100 hours to complete A1; cumulative hours A2 160-200, B1 360-400, B2 560-650 (search summary only). — [Alliance Française Leeds FAQ](https://afleeds.org.uk/faq); [Alliance Française Brussels course structure](https://alliancefr.be/en/learn-french/course-structure)
- Cambridge English: about 90-100 guided learning hours for A1, "as a guideline only", and roughly 200 guided hours to move up one CEFR level (search summary only). — [Cambridge English guided learning hours](https://support.cambridgeenglish.org/hc/en-gb/articles/202838506-Guided-learning-hours)
- LanguageCert puts A1 at about 95 hours (search summary only). — [LanguageCert](https://www.languagecert.org/Guided-Learning-Hours)
- FSI: Category I languages take 24-30 weeks (600-750 class hours) to professional working proficiency; French is listed at 30 weeks; a separate FSI-derived list shows Spanish at 24 weeks (search summary only). — [US State Department, archived](https://webarchive.library.unt.edu/web/20210105072258/https://www.state.gov/foreign-language-training)
- Anki: the default is 20 new cards a day, and at that rate "you can expect your daily reviews to be roughly about 200 cards/day"; reduce new cards to reduce the review burden (search summary only). — [Anki manual, deck options](https://docs.ankiweb.net/deck-options.html)
- Duolingo-funded study (Jiang et al. 2021, Foreign Language Annals 54(4), n = 225 US adults using only Duolingo): measured ACTFL reading and listening of learners who finished the beginning-level Spanish or French content (search summary only). — [NAU publication record](https://experts.nau.edu/en/publications/evaluating-the-reading-and-listening-outcomes-of-beginning-level-/)
- Duolingo's own press release frames that result as reaching four university semesters of reading and listening "in less than half the time". This is company framing; it covers reading and listening only, not speaking or writing. — [Duolingo investor release](https://investors.duolingo.com/news-releases/news-release-details/leading-language-research-journal-publishes-study-showing)

### Inferences
- Arithmetic: 80 hours (midpoint of 60-100) at 15 min/day is 320 days; at 20 min/day, 240 days; at 30 min/day, 160 days. A realistic promise is "A1 in about 6-10 months at 20 minutes a day". Self-study without a teacher or speaking partner may take longer than guided hours, so present this as an estimate.
- Review load: applying Anki's 10x rule, 8 new items a day implies about 80 reviews a day at steady state. At 6-8 seconds per review that is 8-11 minutes, leaving 5-8 minutes for new material in a 15-20 minute session. 20 new items a day (about 200 reviews) does not fit a short session.
- 600-700 items at 6-8 new per weekday takes roughly 16-23 weeks to introduce, well inside the 6-10 month window, leaving the remaining months for grammar, listening and consolidation.
- Proposed session (15-20 min): 1) due reviews, mixed formats, capped at about 60-80 items; 2) one small new chunk: 5-8 words plus one grammar point with 3-4 example sentences; 3) a short applied task (mini-dialogue cloze, dictation of 3 sentences, or a 20-40 word typed message, which mirrors the DELF A1 writing task); 4) a one-line summary of what is due tomorrow. Hard-cap reviews and roll the remainder over so a missed day does not create a punishing backlog.

### Gaps
- No Duolingo (or other) research on optimal session length was found; the "session length" part of the question is unanswered by sources. Hours and ACTFL levels from Jiang et al. 2021 were not extracted.
- Nation's recommended words-per-day rate was not found.
- Hour estimates are for guided/classroom learning; I found no figure specific to app-only self-study to A1.

## 5. Pitfalls of gamified language apps and how to counter them

### Takeaway
The documented risk is that game mechanics become the goal: streaks are valued over the activity itself and are held up by loss aversion, not by perceived learning. Good designs reward demonstrated recall, not time or taps, and make a missed day cheap.

### Cited Findings
- A qualitative study of Duolingo forum discussions describes "gamification misuse": badges, points and leaderboards can be actively harmful to learning when misapplied, not just ineffective (search summary only). — ["When Gamification Spoils Your Learning", arXiv 2203.16175](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2203.16175)
- A Journal of Consumer Research study, as reported in a commentary essay, found users often view extending a streak as more important than the underlying activity (secondary source; search summary only). — [The Decision Lab, "Streak Creep"](https://thedecisionlab.com/insights/consumer-insights/streak-creep-the-perils-of-too-much-gamification)
- A Lund University student thesis found that, unlike habit, streak behaviour is not significantly driven by perceived learning value (student thesis; search summary only). — [Lund University thesis](https://lup.lub.lu.se/student-papers/record/9238652/file/9238707.pdf)
- A practitioner blog argues the streak counter works through loss aversion more than positive motivation; it gives no citation for its abandonment claims, so treat as opinion (search summary only). — [DEV Community post](https://dev.to/pocket_linguist/why-duolingos-gamification-works-and-when-it-doesnt-1d4)

### Inferences
Countermeasures (my proposals, linked to section 3 evidence where possible):
- Recognition-only practice: require typed recall for an item to count as learned; multiple choice never grants mastery (Karpicke & Roediger; Nakata).
- XP farming: award XP for successful spaced recalls of due items and for first-time mastery, with zero or sharply reduced XP for repeating already-easy content the same day. Cap daily XP from reviews.
- Streak anxiety: count "days practised this week" or allow automatic rest days/freeze tokens; never show loss-framed warnings; after a gap, shrink the review queue to a short recovery session.
- Leaderboards: omit them (the dashboard is single-operator anyway) and show personal retention and words-known curves.
- Honest progress: show "items you can produce from memory" separately from "items seen".
- For this repo's XP/realm system: tie realm progress to unit checkpoints that use only productive tasks (typed translation, dictation, 40-word message).

### Gaps
- No 2024-2026 empirical studies on streak anxiety or on recognition-versus-production criticism of specific apps were found. Evidence here is one qualitative paper, one student thesis and commentary, so this section is weaker than sections 2-3.

## 6. Tricky points for English speakers at A1

### Takeaway
The A1 syllabi themselves single out agreement (gender/number) in both languages, ser/estar/hay in Spanish, and negation and question forms in French; most other "known pain points" below are standard teaching knowledge that I did not source in this run.

### Cited Findings
- French: the official DELF A1 guidance tells candidates to watch masculine/feminine and singular/plural agreement (search summary only). — [France Éducation International](https://www.france-education-international.fr/diplome/delf-tout-public/niveau-a1)
- French: A1 negation includes ne... pas and ne... jamais, and "pas" alone in speech (the ne is dropped in spoken French); three competing question forms are all A1; partitive articles and "pas de" are A1. — [digiSchool](https://www.digischool.fr/articles/fle/grammaire-delf-a1-a2-b1-b2/)
- Spanish: A1 splits ser (identification, origin, profession, material, time) from estar (location, bien/mal), and separately contrasts estar (definite/known things) with hay (indefinite/new things). — [UCM A1 grammar programme](https://www.ucm.es/data/cont/media/www/pag-41464/a1_gramatica.pdf)
- Spanish: usted/ustedes are part of the A1 subject-pronoun paradigm; subject pronouns are normally omitted and appear for emphasis (A1.2); gustar-type verbs with article are A1; por qué vs porque is an explicit A1.2 point; para + infinitive is A1.1. — [UCM](https://www.ucm.es/data/cont/media/www/pag-41464/a1_gramatica.pdf)
- Spanish: gender has listed exceptions even at A1 (invariable nouns, heteronyms, feminine nouns in -o from abbreviations, día as masculine). — [UCM](https://www.ucm.es/data/cont/media/www/pag-41464/a1_gramatica.pdf)

### Inferences
Standard teaching points to write as short explicit notes in the pack (not sourced in this run; a native-speaker or teacher review is advisable):

French
- Gender is largely arbitrary: always store and test nouns with their article (un/une), never bare.
- Silent final letters (most final -e, -s, -t, -d, -x, and the -ent verb ending); parle/parles/parlent sound the same. Audio matters more than in Spanish.
- Liaison and elision: les amis with a /z/ link; je + ai becomes j'ai; le/la become l' before a vowel.
- Nasal vowels and the French u vs ou contrast; the uvular r.
- tu vs vous: vous for strangers, older people and service situations, and also for any plural "you".
- ne... pas wraps the verb; ne is dropped in casual speech; un/une/des become de after negation.
- Age and states use avoir (j'ai 30 ans, j'ai faim), not être.
- Numbers 70-99 (soixante-dix, quatre-vingts, quatre-vingt-dix).
- Contractions au/aux/du/des; c'est vs il est.

Spanish
- ser vs estar: both mean "to be"; teach by use cases with contrast pairs, and interleave.
- Gender mostly follows -o/-a, with common exceptions (el día, el problema, la mano).
- tú vs usted, and regional difference: vosotros in Spain versus ustedes in Latin America. The pack must choose a variety (es-ES or es-MX) and say so.
- Inverted ¿ and ¡ at the start of questions and exclamations; accents that change meaning (sí/si, tú/tu, él/el, qué/que).
- Pronunciation: j (like a strong h), ll (like y in most varieties), ñ, single tap r vs trilled rr, silent h, five pure vowels, b and v sounding alike, c/z differing between Spain and Latin America.
- Subject pronouns are usually dropped (hablo, not yo hablo).
- gustar is "it pleases me": me gusta el café / me gustan los libros.
- tener for age and states (tengo 30 años, tengo hambre).
- por vs para basics: para for purpose/destination/recipient, por for cause/duration/exchange; at A1 teach para + infinitive and a few fixed por phrases (por favor, por la mañana).
- Personal a before a person as direct object.

### Gaps
- I did not find or read contrastive-analysis or error-frequency research ranking these difficulties for English speakers; the lists above are conventional, not evidence-ranked.

## 7. Web Speech API (speechSynthesis) support for fr-FR and es-ES/es-MX in 2026

### Takeaway
speechSynthesis exists in all major browsers, but which French and Spanish voices are present depends on the operating system and installed voice packs, and Chrome desktop's Google voices need a network connection, so an offline pack cannot rely on it: feature-detect, filter voices by language at runtime, and degrade gracefully (or ship recorded audio).

### Cited Findings
- MDN lists SpeechSynthesis as "Baseline: widely available", available across browsers since September 2018. — [MDN, SpeechSynthesis](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/SpeechSynthesis)
- Voices load asynchronously: call getVoices() and also listen for the voiceschanged event, because the list may be empty at first. — [MDN](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/SpeechSynthesis)
- Chrome desktop: 19 preloaded Google voices across 15 languages; all require online access with no offline fallback; utterances longer than about 14 seconds are cut off; no boundary events. — [Readium, "SpeechSynthesis in browsers and OSes"](https://readium.org/speech/docs/WebSpeech.html)
- Edge desktop: over 250 "natural" voices across 75 languages (as of April 2024); they require online access; pitch is not supported. — [Readium](https://readium.org/speech/docs/WebSpeech.html)
- Firefox desktop: ships no voices of its own and uses system voices; on macOS, newly installed voices appear only after a reboot. — [Readium](https://readium.org/speech/docs/WebSpeech.html)
- Safari macOS and iOS/iPadOS: preloaded and downloadable voices across 45 languages; downloadable voices may not appear in getVoices(); installing higher-quality variants can make preloaded voices disappear; every voice reports default = true; low-quality Eloquence and novelty voices should be filtered out; Siri voices are not exposed. — [Readium](https://readium.org/speech/docs/WebSpeech.html)
- iOS: all browsers use Safari's engine, so Chrome and Firefox on iOS behave like Safari; a developer report says getVoices() returns only a subset of installed voices (search summary only). — [Apple Developer Forums](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/723503)
- Android Chrome: about 67 high-quality voices on stock Android (April 2024), but only the system language and some popular languages are preloaded; getVoices() can list languages with no installed voice pack, and those fall back to an English voice; language codes may use underscores (fr_FR, es_ES). Samsung Internet also uses underscores. — [Readium](https://readium.org/speech/docs/WebSpeech.html)
- Firefox for Android uses three-letter language codes with a suffix (e.g. eng-US-f000); Edge on Android returns an empty voice list. — [Readium](https://readium.org/speech/docs/WebSpeech.html)
- Conflicting reports on Firefox for Android: one source says it does not implement speech synthesis, while caniuse lists a recent Firefox for Android version as supporting a related feature (search summary only). — [caniuse, SpeechSynthesis](https://caniuse.com/mdn-api_speechsynthesis)
- Synchronous getVoices() returns an empty array on Chrome desktop and Chrome Android; the common pattern is to wait for voiceschanged if the list is empty (search summary only). — [DEV Community, "Cross browser speech synthesis"](https://dev.to/jankapunkt/cross-browser-speech-synthesis-the-hard-way-and-the-easy-way-353)

### Inferences
- Implementation checklist: check `'speechSynthesis' in window`; load voices via getVoices() plus voiceschanged with a timeout; normalize `voice.lang` (lowercase, replace `_` with `-`, compare the first two letters, then region); prefer `localService === true` voices for offline use; let the learner pick and remember a voice; set `utterance.lang` explicitly even when no matching voice is found; keep utterances short (single words and sentences are far below Chrome's roughly 14-second cut-off); trigger speech from a tap, since mobile browsers commonly block audio without a user gesture (not confirmed by a source in this run).
- The Android "falls back to an English voice" behaviour is the worst failure for a language app: French text read by an English voice teaches wrong pronunciation. If no voice whose normalized lang starts with fr or es is found, hide the audio button and show a notice instead of speaking.
- Spanish variety: match es-ES or es-MX/es-US per the pack's chosen variety, falling back to any es-* voice.
- TTS rate around 0.8-0.9 for beginners, with a "slow" button.
- For a truly offline, consistent experience, pre-recorded or pre-generated audio files for the 600-700 items and example sentences are more reliable than speechSynthesis; use speechSynthesis as the zero-cost default and audio files as an optional upgrade.

### Gaps
- No source confirmed, platform by platform, that fr-FR, es-ES and es-MX voices are present by default; the Readium document does not break availability down by these locales. French and Spanish are major languages and are very likely among the defaults on Windows, macOS, iOS and Chrome's Google voices, but that is unverified here.
- The most detailed source (Readium) is dated April 2024; I found nothing describing changes specific to 2025-2026, and I could not read MDN's per-browser version table.
- Voice quality for French liaison and Spanish rr was not evaluated by any source found.
